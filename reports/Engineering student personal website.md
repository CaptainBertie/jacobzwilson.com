# Prove one real project, not polish

A UTS engineering student should build a small, fast, plainly designed site whose job is to show three to five real projects as evidence: what you personally did, what you measured, and what failed. The first screen needs to work in well under a minute, because the only named engineering hiring manager in the evidence opens the portfolio before the resume, and reviewers of portfolios are described as skimming each page for 30 seconds to a minute ([Trossen Robotics](https://www.trossenrobotics.com/post/how-not-to-get-a-job-as-a-mechanical-engineer); [MIT CommLab](https://mitcommlab.mit.edu/meche/commkit/portfolio/)). The case for a site is strongest for this student's hardware work, because photos, CAD, video and test data do not fit on a resume or in a GitHub README. It is weaker for the software work, where GitHub already carries most of the proof. The evidence base is thin: there is no rigorous study of how often recruiters open a graduate engineer's site, how long they stay, or whether having one raises interview rates. What exists is one named hardware manager, a small 2021 survey of React-focused hirers, university career-centre advice, and a lot of vendor blogs. The practical conclusions below are therefore consistent advice, not measured effects. The technical build matters far less than the content: a free static host, a custom domain, and a repeatable project template beat any framework choice. The biggest controllable risks are broken or stale content, generic wording that looks AI-made, and publishing assessed work in a way UTS treats as an academic-integrity problem.

## What hirers say they check, and how little is proven

Start with what is actually known. Matt, a hiring manager for mechanical engineers at Trossen Robotics, says his team opens the portfolio first to judge project fit and then reads the resume, and that applications with no portfolio are deleted, which he says is about half of them ([Trossen Robotics](https://www.trossenrobotics.com/post/how-not-to-get-a-job-as-a-mechanical-engineer)). He wants many images with short text, complex multi-part designs rather than brackets and bolts, a link that works, and a site that does not bury projects behind flashy navigation. He also says a portfolio "is not a place to showcase your web dev skills", and he prefers a simple PDF. That is one manager at one robotics company, so it is a vivid anecdote, not a norm. Its value for this student is directional: for hardware roles, evidence of real builds outranks site engineering.

The only direct survey of whether hirers look at junior personal sites is dated and narrow. In 2021 Johannes Kettmann of Profy.dev gathered about 60 responses from recruiters and React team leads; an overwhelming majority said they would look at a junior's website, yet the most common answer to whether a candidate without one had lower chances was "it depends" ([DEV Community](https://dev.to/profydev/this-survey-among-60-hiring-managers-reveals-don-t-waste-your-time-on-a-react-portfolio-website-17ge)). The sample was self-selected and frontend-specific, with no percentages or time-on-site. Its listed red flags were poor design, broken or outdated content, and, according to one commenter, overengineered sites. The best reading is that a good site may help and a bad or neglected one can hurt, which should shape priorities: reliability and clarity first, novelty last.

Software and hardware readers differ. An engineer-focused practitioner guide argues that frontend and design recruiters click through often while backend, infrastructure and data engineers' sites are rarely opened because LinkedIn and GitHub cover the same ground ([techinterview.org](https://www.techinterview.org/post/3233474627/personal-website-portfolio-engineers/)). That source is a single unsourced blog, so it is opinion. Applied to this student, it suggests the trading bots and Android app should lean on well-documented repos with the website as a guided tour, while the Arduino builds, printed mechanisms and gear CAD, which cannot be read as code, need the site to do the main work. No evidence addresses electrical or embedded hiring managers specifically, so schematics, board photos and demo video are the natural equivalents by inference only.

For Australia, the gap is stark. I found no survey, Engineers Australia statement, Prosple or GradConnection report, or UTS Careers guidance on whether recruiters open engineering graduates' personal sites. Randstad Australia recommends two or three projects, interactive models, a hub page and a LinkedIn Featured link, but it is a recruiter firm with a commercial interest and cites no data ([Randstad Australia](https://www.randstad.com.au/career-advice/career-tips/online-portfolio-engineering-jobs/)). UTS Careers calls an online showcase important for creative fields and LinkedIn management a must ([UTS Careers Blog](https://careersblog.uts.edu.au/job-search-undergraduate/)). A reasonable inference is that large graduate programs run structured online applications where a website is a supplement, so the realistic roles for the site are the resume-plus link for internships, the LinkedIn Featured item, and the thing a hiring manager opens before an interview. The student could settle this cheaply by asking UTS Careers or an engineering industry liaison directly.

One newer pressure deserves attention. Australian HR coverage reports vendor-sourced findings that hirers increasingly treat portfolios as possibly AI-generated, and advises cross-checking LinkedIn, GitHub and portfolio and asking candidates to explain decisions live ([HCAmag AU](https://www.hcamag.com/au/news/general/candidate-fraud-the-warning-signs-hr-should-watch-out-for/573881)). Commentators conclude hirers react to generic content, not AI use itself ([tech.co](https://tech.co/news/half-job-seekers-using-ai)). The defensible inference is that evidence which is hard to fake, such as photos of physical builds, test data, dated history and a candid "what I decided and why", turns a site from a claim into verification. This also fits the student's mix well, since a printed gear train with a measured backlash figure is difficult to counterfeit.

## A site that serves one story and several audiences

The student-side problem is coherence: hardware and software, plus basketball and marketing coursework, can read as scattered. Sources favour a "T-shaped" profile of one deeper area plus breadth, though that is practitioner opinion and one source calls it a poor early-career strategy ([DEV](https://dev.to/alexkondov/the-t-shaped-engineer-3io3); [Work Smarter](https://worksmarter.beehiiv.com/p/depth-vs-breadth)). MIT advises keeping a master set of projects, sending a tailored subset per application, and making web portfolios sortable by skill or topic ([MIT CommLab](https://mitcommlab.mit.edu/meche/commkit/portfolio/)). My synthesis, flagged as inference, is one connecting thread (systems that sense, decide and act, from firmware to trading logic), one headline reused on the site, LinkedIn, GitHub and the CV, and project tags that let a hardware reader and a software reader each find their subset. Sport earns one line in the About text, not a section, and the same goes for marketing unless it ties to product thinking.

The first screen should be plain. Nielsen Norman Group's eyetracking research found people scan in an F-shaped pattern rather than read, so key facts belong top-left in information-carrying words ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content-discovered/)). Its About-page research, which concerns organisations rather than individuals, found that users want plain-language explanations, real photos instead of stock, human copy and easy-to-find contact options ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/about-us-information-on-websites/)). Transferring that to a person is an extrapolation, but the transferable parts are cheap: name, one-line positioning, two or three strongest projects, a CV link and contact, all visible on a phone without scrolling. The UGA career centre's About guide adds a short, concrete bio with a hint of personality and a call to action, refreshed each semester ([UGA](https://career.uga.edu/portfolios_about_me)). Skill bars, mission statements and "passionate about" lines are the commonly cited anti-patterns, though only in opinion sources.

Scope should grow with the degree. A first or second-year site needs one or two finished projects, a short About and contact; later years add team projects with the role stated, and graduation adds the CV, internships and a dated "seeking" line. That staging is my inference, not sourced. A blog is optional and risky: the single practitioner source says most are abandoned within a year and that three strong posts beat thirty weak ones ([techinterview.org](https://www.techinterview.org/post/3233474627/personal-website-portfolio-engineers/)). A post-mortem of a trading bot that doubles as project evidence is a better use than a general blog. The same source warns an outdated site is worse than none, which matches the Profy.dev red flag and one verified example of the failure mode: a robotics portfolio cited by The Construct now resolves to a parked, for-sale domain ([The Construct](https://www.theconstruct.ai/?p=47806)).

Three student-specific constraints deserve more weight than they usually get. First, UTS treats "sharing your assessment or parts of your assessment on any external platforms including online" as cheating ([UTS](https://www.uts.edu.au/current-students/support/academic-support/academic-integrity/what-plagiarism-and-cheating)), and a UTS Law page warns against uploading work to shared-content sites because it may make you liable for facilitating other students' cheating ([UTS Law](https://www.uts.edu.au/for-students/current-students/managing-your-course/current-students-information-law/assessment/academic-misconduct)). That is a Law-faculty page, and I found nothing specific to Engineering, capstone or portfolios. UTS's IP policy says students normally own what they create, with exceptions for collaborative research-program work and use of UTS or third-party background IP ([UTS IP Policy](https://www.uts.edu.au/about/leadership-governance/policies/a-z/intellectual-property-policy)), which the source notes was seen only as a search snippet. The conservative practice, supported by two verified example sites that publicly withhold full assessed reports, is to publish design reasoning, photos, your own CAD, measurements and lessons, withhold submitted reports and code solutions, and ask the subject coordinator before posting group or capstone work. Cornell's course even requires teams to state whether the work may be published, a pattern worth requesting ([Hunter Adams, Cornell](https://vanhunteradams.com/DE1/Final_Project/Final_Project.html)). Second, trading bots raise employer and strategy-sensitivity questions that no source covers; show architecture, risk controls and testing method rather than proprietary parameters, and describe results as what happened, with caveats. Third, privacy: keep home address and personal phone off public pages, use suburb-level location, and strip photo location data ([PEN America manual](https://onlineharassmentfieldmanual.pen.org/?p=333)). A work-rights line is something Australian recruiters look for, but that is my inference, unsourced.

## How a project page should read, and the examples that show it

Advice from hiring-side and teaching sources converges on structure. Trossen asks for CAD screenshots, photos or video of builds, your specific contribution in team projects, the challenges and iterations, and personal work beyond coursework, and says a simple PDF or GitHub page is acceptable ([Trossen portfolio guidelines](https://www.trossenrobotics.com/portfolio-guidelines)). A Cornell embedded-systems course gives the strongest institutional template: a one-sentence summary, design rationale and tradeoffs, hardware and program design including **failed attempts**, results with test data, conclusions against expectations, and appendices ([Hunter Adams, Cornell](https://vanhunteradams.com/DE1/Final_Project/Final_Project.html)). Vendor sources agree on 3 to 7 projects, strongest first, fewer well documented rather than many thin ([Vista Projects](https://www.vistaprojects.com/mechanical-engineering-portfolio-examples/); [The Construct](https://www.theconstruct.ai/?p=47806); [Wix](https://www.wix.com/blog/how-to-make-an-engineering-portfolio)). The counts differ slightly because the sources are commercial and unscientific, so the useful range is three to five.

I opened and checked fourteen real student and early-career sites. Notes: the fetch tool returned summarised text, not rendered pages, so design polish and mobile behaviour are untested, and I found no Reddit or Hacker News thread naming student hardware portfolios as models, so none of these is "widely praised". Selection is mine. Two stand out. Jake Cummings, a UT Tyler mechanical engineering student, has a water-rocket page that states his role and team context, shows a before-and-after table (for example 21.2 to 34 m/s velocity and 3.43 to 5.12 s flight time), includes photos of a shattered PLA nose cone and failed prints, lists CAD revisions, and notes that the assessed report is withheld ([Cummings](https://jakecummings-dev.pages.dev/projects/water-rocket/)). Even there, a small percentage mismatch and caption errors were noted, a reminder that sloppy numbers undercut trust. Nhan Dang, a UTS mechanical engineering honours student, uses a numbered template (Overview, Problem, My Role, Process, Key Decisions, Material, Outcome, Next Steps) with a facts sidebar of year, status and tools, and marks the work as prototype-stage ([Dang](https://tranthunhan.github.io/projects/confined-space-inspection-robot.html)). It has no measurements or concrete failures, so its claims stay generic. Combining Dang's template with Cummings's measured table and failure photos is the best pattern I saw, and it is directly comparable because Dang attends the same university.

The weaker sites fail in repeatable ways: bare title lists on the homepage, "coming soon" links that go nowhere, impact percentages with no baseline, no failures shown, and team work not separated from own work. A Toronto FSAE site gives year-by-year cards with role, result and one-line impact, a good pattern, but its figures such as 15 percent weight reduction have no method behind them ([Cao](https://portfolio-website-dku.pages.dev/)). A BCIT mechatronics site visibly labels two projects as failures with written reflection, though its capstone claims are projections ([Nguyen](https://trungkhang111005.github.io)). A UNSW student's software-only site has live demos but "case study (soon)" links that go to a placeholder ([Nguyen, UNSW](https://website-6sn.pages.dev/)). Fixing the common faults alone would put a site ahead of most of the sample.

For this student's mix, the evidence supports tailoring the proof to the type of work, though most of the following is inference. Each hardware page needs a short muted video of the thing working, a CAD view or annotated render, and one number with its method, such as measured against designed gear backlash and the cause of the gap. Hiring-side sources say a render alone is insufficient and that an embedded working demo is more convincing than a static image ([The Construct](https://www.theconstruct.ai/?p=47806)), and Trossen notes no manager will download a folder of CAD to open it. For electronics, real schematics viewable without a paid tool are better than prose ([Built In](https://builtin.com/hardware/hardware-engineering-portfolio)), though that article is promotional. Software pages need an architecture diagram, a README-quality repo, an embedded demo and a measured outcome, with tests and tooling named. The Android app and web apps suit live demos or screen recordings; the data pipeline and bots suit architecture diagrams plus honest performance statements.

## The site as an engineering artefact

The build choice carries less signal than the content. I found no study comparing hirer reactions to coded sites versus builder sites, and the only relevant statements concern generic content and slow or confusing sites. Every hosted builder needs a paid plan for a custom domain, and published prices conflict between sources (Framer, Webflow, Squarespace and Wix are quoted anywhere from about $10 to $25 a month), so check vendor pages before choosing ([toolradar](https://toolradar.com/guides/best-portfolio-website-builders)). The lock-in point, that coded sites are portable and builder designs generally are not, is my inference. A static site generator such as Astro, with Markdown or MDX project files validated by a schema, fits one author without a CMS ([Astro docs](https://docs.astro.build/en/guides/content-collections/)). Hosting is effectively free: GitHub Pages has a 1 GB site limit and bars commercial use ([GitHub Pages limits](https://docs.github.com/en/pages/getting-started-with-github-pages/github-pages-limits)), Vercel Hobby carries a 100 GB transfer guideline ([Vercel](https://vercel.com/docs/limits/fair-use-guidelines)), and Cloudflare Pages allows 25 MiB per asset ([Cloudflare](https://developers.cloudflare.com/pages/platform/limits/)). That per-file cap, by my inference, pushes large video and raw CAD exports to a video host or object storage.

Performance has firm targets. Google's Core Web Vitals "good" thresholds are largest contentful paint within 2.5 seconds, interaction to next paint 200 milliseconds or less, and layout shift 0.1 or less at the 75th percentile ([web.dev](https://web.dev/articles/vitals)). Images are the largest element on roughly three quarters of mobile pages according to a Web Almanac figure I could only read through secondary summaries, so the hero image of each project page is the first thing to optimise. For video, the documented pattern replaces GIFs with muted, looping, inline video in WebM and MP4, and loads YouTube or Vimeo through a click-to-load facade ([web.dev video](https://web.dev/articles/lazy-loading-video); [lite-youtube-embed](https://github.com/paulirish/lite-youtube-embed)). For modern image formats, WebP and AVIF are supported in all current browsers ([web.dev](https://web.dev/articles/choose-the-right-image-format)). I found no citable bitrate or file-size targets, so any numbers beyond these are judgement.

Interactive 3D is possible but conditional. Onshape and SolidWorks export glTF or GLB natively, while Fusion has no native export and relies on paid plugins or an OBJ or FBX route through Blender ([SolidWorks help](https://help.solidworks.com/2025/English/SolidWorks/sldworks/t_export_using_extended_reality.htm); [Autodesk forum](https://forums.autodesk.com/t5/fusion-design-validate-document/exporting-fusion-files-to-glb-format/m-p/11389465)). Google's model-viewer shows GLB with poster images and lazy loading ([modelviewer.dev](https://modelviewer.dev/docs/index.html)), and Sketchfab's free tier allows embedding with a 100 MB per-model limit ([Sketchfab](https://sketchfab.com/plans?ref=header)). Given the hardware evidence above, a 3D viewer is a nice extra for the gear CAD, not a requirement, and a drag-only viewer needs a non-drag alternative to meet WCAG 2.2's dragging criterion ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/WAI/standards-guidelines/wcag/new-in-22/)). That accessibility standard also sets a 24 by 24 pixel minimum target size, which matters on phones. I found no current data on how many recruiters view on mobile; old LinkedIn figures put mobile at 38 percent of unique visitors in 2013 and about 60 percent by 2016 ([Statista](https://www.statista.com/statistics/425224/linkedin-mobile-unique-visitors-share)), and 2026 claims conflict. Building mobile-first is still prudent because a LinkedIn link is often opened in the phone app, but that is inference.

Discoverability and polish are small items. LinkedIn link previews need og:title, og:image, og:description and og:url, with an image at least 1200 by 627 pixels ([LinkedIn Help](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a521928)). Google says no special schema or optimisation is needed to appear in AI features, though Person structured data helps disambiguate a name ([Google](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/ai-features); [Google ProfilePage](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/profile-page)). For the domain, a .com.au is for commercial entities, while a personal .au name or .id.au is open to individuals with Australian presence, and eligibility for visa holders is unconfirmed in the sources ([auDA](https://auda.org.au/au-domain-names/domain-name-help/comau-and-netau-rules-eligibility-and-allocation)). Wholesale .au pricing is A$9.50 a year ex GST ([auDA pricing](https://auda.org.au/registrar-resources/wholesale-au-domain-pricing)). Email forwarding on the domain is free through Cloudflare, but sending from it is disputed in the guides I found. A plain mailto link avoids a third-party script, and honeypot or Turnstile spam filtering is the usual escalation if a form is wanted. For the resume PDF, name the file with your name and role, omit the street address, and strip metadata, per career and privacy blogs ([VisualCV](https://visualcv.com/blog/how-to-name-your-resume-file); [Total Defense](https://www.totaldefense.com/security-blog/remove-your-home-address-from-your-resume-to-protect-your-privacy-while-job-hunting/)).

## Where the evidence is too weak to lean on

Three gaps shape how far to trust the advice above. No controlled evidence shows that a personal site raises interview rates, and the one direct survey is five years old and React-specific. Every project-writing recommendation comes from a teaching rubric, a career blog or a vendor, so "include a failure and one measured number" is better described as convergent expert opinion than a tested method. And no Australian source speaks to this question at all. Several figures circulate widely and could not be traced to a primary study: the oft-quoted share of hiring managers who open portfolio links, portfolio dwell times in the seconds or minutes range attributed to a "NACE study", and a cluster of percentages about portfolios on AI-written blogs. This report leaves them out. The 7.4-second resume-scan figure from a 2018 Ladders eye-tracking study ([HR Dive](https://www.hrdive.com/news/eye-tracking-study-shows-recruiters-look-at-resumes-for-7-seconds/541582/)) is real but vendor-run, thinly documented, and concerns resumes, not websites. Finally, the example sites were read as text, never rendered, so claims about visual quality, speed and phone layout in them are untested.

## Conclusion

The research changes the question from "how impressive should my site look" to "how quickly can a stranger verify that I built something real". That reframing favours the student's strengths: printed mechanisms, gear CAD and firmware generate photos, video and measurements that are hard to counterfeit, and a trading-bot post-mortem shows the kind of judgement that rubrics explicitly ask for. It also warns against the usual temptations, which are a clever framework, an animated hero, a blog with no plan to update it and unpublished assessed material.

Two cheap steps would resolve most of the uncertainty the sources leave open. Before publishing any coursework or capstone, ask each subject coordinator in writing what UTS allows, since no engineering-specific rule was found. And before investing heavily, ask UTS Careers or a target employer whether graduate screeners open personal sites at all, since Australian evidence is absent. Until then, the low-cost default is one project template, three to five finished pages, a PDF resume, a LinkedIn Featured link and a site small enough to keep current.
